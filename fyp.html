Abstract

This study estimates the causal effect of an environmental awareness intervention on sustainable transport attitudes and behavioural intentions among undergraduate students in Karachi, Pakistan. Grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior and Awareness Theory, it applies a two-period difference-in-differences (DiD) design to 800 student-observations collected across two survey rounds at IBA and IoBM, with treatment assigned within each university to remove campus-level confounding. Four outcomes are examined: e-bike willingness and affordability, general willingness for sustainable transport, definition-based adoption, and willingness to switch in response to traffic congestion. Demographic, attitudinal, structural and environmental control blocks are added cumulatively alongside university fixed effects, with heteroskedasticity-robust inference throughout.

The intervention has a positive, significant effect on congestion-anchored willingness (β = +0.425, p = 0.015), while effects on the abstract, affordability-constrained and definitional outcomes are insignificant in every specification. The effect is concentrated among male students (β = +0.530, p = 0.024) and students living far from campus (β = +0.487, p = 0.061). Awareness campaigns work best when anchored to a concrete local friction such as congestion, and paired with structural enablers, rather than relying on abstract climate messaging.

Design and estimation

Round 1 (baseline) produced 462 responses; Round 2, after the intervention was delivered to the treatment group, produced 338. Roughly half of respondents in each university were treated.

Yist = β₀ + β₁Treati + β₂Postt + β₃(Treati×Postt) + γDXD + γAXA + γSXS + γEXE + αs + εist

  • β₃ is the average treatment effect on the treated under parallel trends.
  • Controls, added cumulatively: demographic (age, gender, distance, job status, own-pocket spending) → attitudinal (peer encouragement, prestige, habit, climate seriousness, environmental engagement) → structural (car ownership, reliability, fare, flexibility, trips) → environmental (weather, congestion, infrastructure satisfaction).
  • Diagnostics: heteroskedasticity tests with robust SEs; attrition balance test on 62 Round-1 dropouts (smallest p = 0.42, consistent with random attrition); robustness across specifications.

Policy implications

The intervention did not shift transport attitudes across the board. It raised the salience of a daily, tangible condition (traffic congestion) as a reason to switch mode, and the effect sits with the groups most exposed to it. Campaigns should tie environmental messaging to local frictions and be paired with reliable buses, peer carpooling and targeted outreach.

Limitations

Two periods limit power relative to multi-wave panels; subgroup estimates are imprecise at n = 800; the four outcomes are correlated, so multiple-testing corrections would tighten thresholds; treatment was assigned within university rather than randomised at the individual level.
